VIII Temporal hydrogen → two-clock periodic table

Proper time against coordinate time: the atom as a slowed clock

The premise was that an atom could be derived from the relationship between an electron's proper time and the laboratory's coordinate time. It can, exactly. The electron carries its own clock — its Compton phase, ticking at ωC = mc²/ℏ in proper time. A bound state carries a second clock — its energy phase, ticking at E/ℏ in coordinate time. Require them to be the same phase and the energy of every hydrogen-like level is the electron's rest energy slowed by its own time dilation, E = mc² ⟨dτ/dt⟩. The binding energy is the proper time the electron loses; every spectral line is the difference between two clock rates; and the construction holds for one force law only — the inverse square.

Every atom, not one: the periodic table in proper time

One atom was never enough. The premise extends to the whole table, and at the level of the whole atom it stays exact wherever every interaction is inverse-square: nucleus on electron, electron on electron, as in a Dirac–Slater mean field. The same virial argument that locked hydrogen’s energy to its clock also locks any such atom’s total energy to the sum of its electrons’ proper-time deficits, plus a small term from inside the nucleus (at most 0.6%, at oganesson). Correlation functionals, no-pair many-body Hamiltonians, and QED corrections aren’t inverse-square, so the exact ledger leaves them out. Each electron is then a clock plus a shelter: its clock is set by the charge inside its orbit, and the charge outside exerts no force (Newton’s shell theorem), so it can’t change the clock — it only lifts the energy. Below, all 118 elements are computed that way, with nothing fitted to any of them, and graded against published reference values. So are the clocks that end an atom’s states: radiative decay, which runs on the cube of the transition’s period, and alpha decay, which counts how far the escaping particle falls short of the Coulomb speed.

Every element is a self-consistent relativistic atom computed offline, nothing fitted: the whole-atom ledger closes to 1.1×10−7 or better for all 118 elements, ionization energy comes in at 5.0% RMS against 99 NIST values, K-shell binding at 0.46% RMS. The largest misses (8–20%, mostly transition metals) trace to local-density exchange's known d/s-electron imbalance, shown rather than trimmed. The same clock-plus-shelter ledger describes, without resolving, the 4s/3d filling-order paradox: calcium's 3d clock runs 3× slower than its 4s but is 94% sheltered, so 4s still lies lower — by scandium the 3d is occupied yet still 0.75 eV above 4s. [OPEN] Radiative decay runs on the cube of the transition period (hydrogen 2p: 1.598 ns, 0.1% from NIST); alpha decay on the Sommerfeld tunneling exponent, fitting 35 even-even emitters to 0.56 decades RMS across 29.8 decades of half-life with one textbook radius and no fitted constant. Two clocks are enough for the whole construction. There's no fitting, and it's exact only for an inverse-square force. Many-electron atoms enter through X-ray screening constants instead, with the Kα doublet as the cleanest proof, since the non-relativistic atom predicts it to be exactly zero.
