## Temporal hydrogen → two-clock periodic table

> Proper time against coordinate time: *the atom as a slowed clock*

The premise was that an atom could be derived from the relationship between an electron's proper time and the laboratory's coordinate time. It can, exactly. The electron carries its own clock — its Compton phase, ticking at ω_C = mc²/ℏ in *proper* time. A bound state carries a second clock — its energy phase, ticking at E/ℏ in *coordinate* time. Require them to be the same phase and the energy of every hydrogen-like level is the electron's rest energy slowed by its own time dilation, **E = mc² ⟨dτ/dt⟩**. The binding energy is the proper time the electron loses; every spectral line is the difference between two clock rates; and the construction holds for one force law only — the inverse square.

One atom was never enough. The premise extends to the whole table, and at the level of the whole atom it stays exact wherever every interaction is inverse-square: nucleus on electron, electron on electron, as in a Dirac–Slater mean field. The same virial argument that locked hydrogen's energy to its clock also locks *any* such atom's total energy to the sum of its electrons' proper-time deficits, plus a small term from inside the nucleus (at most 0.6%, at oganesson). Correlation functionals, no-pair many-body Hamiltonians, and QED corrections aren't inverse-square, so the exact ledger leaves them out. Each electron is then a clock plus a shelter: its clock is set by the charge inside its orbit, and the charge outside exerts no force (Newton's shell theorem), so it can't change the clock — it only lifts the energy. Below, all 118 elements are computed that way, with nothing fitted to any of them, and graded against published reference values. So are the clocks that end an atom's states: radiative decay, which runs on the **cube** of the transition's period, and alpha decay, which counts how far the escaping particle falls short of the Coulomb speed.

**Every atom, graded.** Each element is a self-consistent relativistic atom computed offline for this module: the radial Dirac equation for every electron, a finite nucleus, and the electrons' mutual field built from their own density. The inputs are Z and the NIST ground configuration, predicted past lawrencium. Nothing is fitted to any atom. The ingredients that are choices, not derivations, are tagged ASSUMED here: the standard local-density treatment of exchange and correlation, the statistical split of each shell into its two j-subshells (jj filling for the 6p and 7p shells), and the departing-electron rule. One electron is removed from each of the three least-bound shells and the smallest ionization energy is kept, so ion ground states that need the remaining electrons rearranged (V⁺ 3d⁴, Co⁺ 3d⁸, Ni⁺ 3d⁹, La⁺ 5d², Ce⁺ 4f5d²) lie outside it. The results: the whole-atom ledger E = Σ mc²(⟨dτ/dt⟩ − 1) + (inside the nucleus) closes to **1.1×10^−7 or better for all 118**, in the exchange-only (Dirac–Slater) version of the same atoms, where every interaction is inverse-square. That is a theorem checked, not a fit. Against the 99 NIST reference values that are not mere estimates (all measured except hydrogen's, which is QED theory anchored to spectroscopy), the first ionization energy comes out with an **RMS error of 5.0% (median 3.2%)**. The departing-electron rule gives the known departing shell where one exists: lutetium, hafnium, yttrium and palladium lose a d electron, and actinium, protactinium, uranium, neptunium and curium lose a 6d. K-shell binding across the 83 elements from neon to uranium comes out at **0.46% RMS**. The residual averages +0.42%. Above Z ≈ 40 it rises smoothly to +0.63% at uranium, the size and sign of the Breit interaction and QED that a Dirac mean field leaves out. Below Z ≈ 40, where those effects are under 0.1%, it reaches +1.2% (sodium), and there it mostly reflects the comparison itself: the Data Booklet references metals to the Fermi level, not a free atom's vacuum level, and the transition state adds its own error. The largest ionization misses, 8–20%, are transition metals (Ni, Cr, Cu, Co, Pd, Nb, Pt, Ag, Ru, Mo, Rh). The N/O inversion of the half-filled shell is reproduced; the P/S and As/Se inversions are not. Local-density exchange is known to get the balance between d and s electrons wrong; for nickel most of the +19.5% has a second cause, its 3d⁹ ion ground state, which one-electron removal cannot reach. These misses are shown rather than trimmed.

**What the ledger says about the periodic table.** Read any row of the ledger and the same shape appears. The electron that ionizes is not a lazy electron. Across the whole table its clock loses between about one second (lithium's 2s) and 36 seconds (copernicium's 6d) of proper time a day. That is half to fifteen times what hydrogen's ground-state electron loses, because it dips through the core where the enclosed charge is large. What makes it loosely held is shelter: the potential of the charge lying outside it cancels about two-thirds to three-quarters of its clock term for the alkali s electrons (66% for Li to 76% for Fr), and up to 97% for palladium's 4d. That charge includes the electron's own cloud. Exact exchange would take that self part back out. Local-density exchange removes only part of it (2.7 of 8.1 eV in hydrogen, where the whole shelter is self), which is why the model's hydrogen 1s clock reads 2.15 s/day against the exact 2.30. The binding of the outer electron is therefore a small residue of a large clock term and a large shelter term, with a smaller exchange–correlation term. That's why chemistry is sensitive. The ledger also describes, without resolving, the most famous oddity of the filling order. In calcium the 3d electron is the *slower* clock of the two — its clock term is 3.05 times the 4s's, because it sits deeper (⟨r⟩ = 2.3 against 4.0 bohr). But 94% of that clock is sheltered, against 75% for the 4s, so the 4s orbital lies 1.7 eV lower. The ordering of the two orbital energies is set by shelter, not by the clock; which orbitals are occupied is decided by total energies. In scandium the 3d has collapsed further inward (clock 80 eV, 95% sheltered) and is occupied, yet it still lies 0.75 eV above the 4s: the familiar 4s/3d paradox, described here but not resolved. [same computed atoms, spin-up channel; the unoccupied 3d is evaluated in calcium's own field] The heavy end sharpens it: the 1s clock of an oganesson atom loses 11.3 hours a day, and from rutherfordium to copernicium the 7s is pulled inward where clocks are slow. So in this model the 6d is the electron that ionizes in every one of them except bohrium (where 7s wins by 0.1 eV). That is a prediction; none of these ions has been measured.

**The decay clocks, and where t³ really lives.** An excited atom radiates on a clock that is literally a cube of time: τ = 3T³/(32π³α t_d²), with T the period of the emitted light and t_d the light-crossing time of the atom's dipole. This is the standard electric-dipole rate, rewritten in time and nothing else (FORCED-BY-MECHANICS). With the measured period it gives hydrogen's 2p lifetime as 1.598 ns, 0.1% from the 1.596 ns theoretical reference (NIST). For the alkali resonance lines, with the dipole taken from the computed atoms and the period from the measured line, it lands within +0.6% (Li), +1.1% (Na), −3.9% (K), −8.0% (Rb) and −8.1% (Cs) of the NIST Handbook lifetimes. The Handbook's Rb value is older; the modern 26.2 ns (Volz & Schmoranzer 1996) would put Rb near −5%. The heavy-alkali shortfall is core polarization, which the mean field leaves out. Because the period enters cubed, a 3% error in the line energy becomes a 9% error in the lifetime; that is why the fully computed version, with the model's own period, drifts to −18%. The cube is also why the excited states of heavy one-electron ions die fastest: T³ falls as Z^−6 while t_d² falls only as Z^−2, so their lifetimes fall as Z^−4, from nanoseconds in hydrogen to 7 attoseconds in the heaviest. Neutral atoms' valence lifetimes do not follow it; the alkalis all sit at 16–31 ns. And α enters the law exactly once, as the coupling between the period's cube and the dipole's square. [OPEN] That is the role the paper assigned to t₃, and the paper's "Scramble Zone", picoseconds to nanoseconds, is where optical spontaneous emission actually sits (1.6 ns for hydrogen, 16–31 ns for the alkalis). Nuclear decays are temporal too, but not cubic. An alpha particle leaves after e^2G ticks of a nuclear clock, t_a = 2R/v ≈ 10^−21 s, the same order as the electron's Compton period. The exponent is set by the Sommerfeld number η = Z_1Z_2α/β, the Coulomb speed over the particle's speed. For a bound electron η is exactly n, its shell; an alpha particle with η ≈ 15–27 is not bound but trapped, and must tunnel. With the textbook radius r₀ = 1.2 fm (ASSUMED) and no fitted constant, 35 even-even emitters from Te-106 to Og-294 come out within **0.56 decades RMS** across **29.8 decades** of half-life. The absolute level depends on the assumed radius (r₀ = 1.15, 1.25 and 1.3 fm give 0.45, 1.11 and 1.70 decades RMS); the scatter about it barely does (0.37–0.46 decades). Po-210 is the one outlier, at −1.6 decades (−1.2 after the overall −0.39-decade offset); it sits on the N = 126 shell closure, where alpha particles are known to be harder to form.

**What the premise now says, and where it stops.** The original construction reached hydrogen's ground state with three fitted times (the table below, kept for the record). The version above fits nothing. Two clocks are enough: the electron's own Compton clock, running in proper time, and the stationary state's phase, running in coordinate time. Locking them gives E = mc²⟨dτ/dt⟩ for every level of every one-electron atom, and the Dirac virial theorem proves the lock exact — exact *only* for an inverse-square force, which is the one force law the atom actually has (FORCED-BY-MECHANICS; the "Why 1/r" view lets any other law fail in front of you). The rest is subtraction. Binding is the proper time the electron loses: 2.30 s per day in hydrogen's ground state, 6.2 hours per day in U⁹¹⁺. A spectral line is the difference of two clock rates, and eq (26) of the cubic-time paper, fed a free electron's clock ω_C and the bound state's clock E/ℏ as its two frequencies, both read on the laboratory clock, beats at exactly B/h. For hydrogen that's the Lyman limit, so the photon that ionises the atom *is* the beat. The framework's gearing s_t : s_τ = 2π : 1 (Module XIII) survives as the free electron's case, and binding is the slip away from it. Real many-electron atoms enter through their X-ray lines with one screening constant each: Moseley's σ = 1 for Kα₁ and Sommerfeld's σ = 3.5 for the doublet, both ASSUMED from the historical literature, neither tuned here. The doublet is the cleanest proof in the module, because the non-relativistic atom predicts it to be exactly zero. Three things lie outside the construction and each panel says where: the Lamb shift (QED, plus the finite nuclear size for heavy ions; every residual above collapses onto it), outer-shell screening (the Kα₁ remainder of −0.9 to +1.5%, mostly +0.4 to +1.5%), and the failure of a single doublet screening constant below Z ≈ 30 (−14% at Al). The chemistry at gold, mercury and lead is the standard heuristic, labelled as one.

**The odd-harmonic law.** The interference view runs the paper's own eq (26) and reads off what it contains. With r = ω₂/ω₁, the carrier is (1+r)/2 and the envelope |1−r|/2, so their ratio is (1+r)/|1−r|. Whenever r is *superparticular*, r = (n+1)/n, that ratio is exactly the odd integer **2n+1** (FORCED-BY-MECHANICS; the algebra is elementary, which is the point — it is not fitted). The paper's two worked examples both sit on this law and neither is remarked on there: the hydrogen row runs at the octave 2:1 → 3:1, and figure 1 runs at the fifth 3:2 → 5:1. Iterating the map from that octave and reducing by octaves gives 2:1 → 3:2 → 5:4 → 9:8 → 17:16 … = (2^k+1)/2^k. The third term is the just major third — and it lands 13.7¢ *flat* of this lattice's tempered cell, three of them falling 41¢ short of the octave. That gap is not a defect in the reading: it is exactly why the M3 lattice tempers, since only the tempered third satisfies Module VII's ratified (2^1/3)³ = 2. The author's DAW observation that the patterns stayed stable "within 100 cents" reads quantitatively here as **beat/carrier ≤ 2.9%**.

**The law is an equivalence, not an implication.** The statement above runs one way: superparticular ⇒ odd. The phase scope's two new locks supply the converse, and it costs nothing. For any a:b *in lowest terms* write d = a − b. Then carrier:beat = (a+b)/|a−b| = **2b/d + 1**. That is an integer exactly when d | 2b, and an *odd* integer exactly when 2b/d is even, i.e. when d | b. But gcd(a,b) = 1, so d | b forces d | a and hence d | 1. Therefore **carrier:beat is an odd integer ⇔ a − b = 1**. The odd-harmonic law does not merely hold for superparticular ratios — it *characterises* them, and no other commensurate ratio can satisfy it (FORCED-BY-MECHANICS). Two locks in the phase scope show the two ways it fails: **5:3** has d = 2 and lands on carrier:beat = 4 (an integer, but an even one), while **8:5** has d = 3 and lands on 13/3, not an integer at all. This also settles what the octave chain is doing: 2:1 → 3:2 → 5:4 → 9:8 is (2^k+1)/2^k, which has d = 1 at every step by construction, so the chain cannot leave the law. It was never at risk along it, and only ratios off the chain can test it.

**A correction to the above, and an exact pair.** On its own domain the map is an *involution*. Take ω₂ > ω₁ so r > 1, where f(r) = (1+r)/(r−1); then f(f(r)) = r for every r, exactly, since (1 + (1+r)/(r−1)) ÷ ((1+r)/(r−1) − 1) = 2r/2. The restriction is not a dodge: naming the faster clock second is a labelling choice, and carrier:beat is invariant under r → 1/r, so r > 1 *is* the whole space. (Written carelessly as (1+r)/|1−r| the identity fails below unison — there (1+r)/(1−r) iterates to −1/r, not r.) So f generates no ladder by itself — it pairs intervals off two at a time, and the 2:1 → 3:2 → 5:4 chain exists only because each output is folded back by octaves before the map is applied again. The walking is done by the octave reduction, not by the interference; the earlier framing gave f more credit than it is owed. What survives intact, and sharper for the correction, is a single exact pair: hydrogen's n = 1 ↔ 3 energy ratio is (3/1)² = **9**, and **f(9) = 10/8 = 5/4** — the just major third, with no reduction needed at all. Two integers, no fitting, and the preset chip puts the engine on it.
